# Apollo 11 Lunar Module Descent Stage: Mission, Engineering, and Legacy

## Overview & Location
The Apollo 11 Lunar Module *Eagle* landed at Tranquility Base in Mare Tranquillitatis on July 20, 1969, at 20:17:40 UTC. The descent stage remained on the lunar surface after the crew departed in the ascent stage.

## Why Was the Descent Stage Left on the Moon?
The Lunar Module consisted of two stages. The descent stage carried the landing gear, descent engine, and propellant used to reach the surface. After the surface mission, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin returned to lunar orbit in the much lighter ascent stage and rendezvoused with the Command and Service Module *Columbia*.

The descent stage was not designed to return to orbit. Leaving it behind reduced the mass that the ascent engine had to lift and was an intentional part of the mission architecture.

## Engineering Architecture & Propulsion
- **Propulsion:** The Descent Propulsion System used hypergolic Aerozine 50 fuel and nitrogen tetroxide oxidizer. Its throttleable engine supported the controlled lunar landing.
- **Landing Gear:** Four landing legs used crushable structures to absorb touchdown energy.
- **Contact Probes:** Probes attached to the footpads helped indicate contact with the lunar surface.

## Scientific Equipment & Lasting Heritage
Apollo 11 deployed the **Early Apollo Scientific Experiments Package (EASEP)**, a simplified precursor to the more comprehensive Apollo Lunar Surface Experiments Packages (ALSEP) carried by later landing missions. EASEP included:
1. **Passive Seismic Experiment Package (PSEP):** Recorded seismic activity on the Moon.
2. **Laser Ranging Retroreflector (LRRR):** Reflected laser pulses sent from Earth, allowing scientists to measure the Earth–Moon distance.
   
The Apollo 11 crew also deployed a solar-wind composition experiment. These instruments were separate from the later, larger ALSEP configuration; the Apollo 11 descent stage should not be described as carrying a full later-mission ALSEP package.

The crew left a commemorative plaque on the descent stage ladder. The landing site is historically significant, and access to it is governed by planetary-protection and heritage considerations; avoid describing it as a formally designated archaeological monument unless a specific designation is cited.
